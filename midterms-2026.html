The basics

What are the midterms?

Millions of Americans are voting in the midterm elections. They are called “midterms” because they happen halfway through a president’s four-year term. Donald Trump is not on the ballot, but the vote is widely seen as a verdict on his presidency. Unlike a presidential election, there is no single winner: voters elect hundreds of representatives, so the result is the sum of many local fights.

The balance of power

Who controls Congress now?

Republicans currently control both chambers. In the House, they hold 218 seats, plus one independent who votes with them, while Democrats hold 214 and two seats are empty. Democrats need at least 218 seats to win a majority. In the Senate, Republicans lead 53 to 47, so Democrats need a net gain of four seats. A 50–50 tie would still favour Republicans, because Vice President JD Vance can cast the deciding vote.

The mood of the country

A difficult climate for Republicans

History is not on the president’s side: his party usually loses seats at midterms. In 2018, during Trump’s first term, Democrats gained 40 seats in the House. This year the polls look similar. A Reuters/Ipsos survey at the end of October gave Trump an approval rating of just 32%, and Democrats led Republicans by 47% to 36% when voters were asked which party they would choose for Congress. Democrats also seem more motivated: 46% of them said they were very enthusiastic about voting, against 30% of Republicans.
